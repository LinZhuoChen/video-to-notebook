2. VAE: Compressing Data into a Latent Space

Primary source: Vizuara Principles of Diffusion Models Lecture 2 (instructor: Dr. Raj Dandekar); secondary: CMU 10-799 Lecture 1.

📌 TL;DR

VAE = Autoencoder + a probability viewpoint + variational inference. It upgrades the "compress-then-reconstruct" bottleneck from a single point to a distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The objective is to maximise the data log-likelihood $\log p(x)$. But $\log p(x) = \log \int p(x|z) p(z) \, dz$ is intractable in high dimensions, so we introduce an approximate posterior $q(z|x)$ (the encoder), and via Jensen's inequality we get the ELBO (Evidence Lower BOund):

$$\log p(x) \;\geq\; \mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] \;-\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(z|x) \,\|\, p(z)\big)$$

Maximising ELBO does two things simultaneously:

  • Reconstruction term: the decoder reconstructs $x$ from $z$ accurately.
  • Regularisation term: the encoder's latent distribution stays close to the prior $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$.

To backprop through the sampling step $z \sim q(z|x)$, we use the reparameterisation trick $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$.

Why open the textbook with VAE? Because DDPM is essentially a $T$-step hierarchical VAE — its training loss is just the ELBO unrolled. Every brick we lay in this chapter (latent variables, variational inference, ELBO, KL, reparameterisation) gets reused.

I. Why start diffusion from VAE?

Dr. Raj opens Lecture 2 by stressing that understanding diffusion requires nailing VAE first. Three reasons:

  1. Same mathematical spine: DDPM's training loss is the ELBO unrolled across $T$ hierarchical steps. If you don't know how to derive ELBO from $\log p(x)$, the DDPM loss equations look like hieroglyphics.
  2. Same idea: VAE's "compress to a latent, then decode back" structure becomes "step-by-step noise to latent, then step-by-step denoise back" in diffusion. Diffusion is VAE unrolled in time.
  3. First principles: vanilla VAE → hierarchical VAE → DDPM. Each step deepens the previous one. Skipping VAE straight to DDPM is dangerous.
The instructor invokes Karpathy: "Whenever you learn something from scratch, you understand the gaps in your knowledge." — this chapter's job is to take the scattered VAE material on the internet and knead it into one 0-to-1 cognitive ramp.

II. Problem setup: 100 students writing "hello"

Dr. Raj's running example throughout Lecture 2:

Suppose your class has 100 students; each writes "hello" on a piece of paper. Every handwriting is different — some slant left, some slant right; some are neat, some are messy; some are cursive, some are not.

Now someone asks you to build a machine: press a button → the machine prints a paper with "hello" on it, in the style of someone in this class.

In formal language:

We want to build a sampler (a machine) whose outputs follow the true probability distribution of how students in this class write "hello."

This is exactly the core goal of deep generative modeling — learn the real data distribution and sample from it.

🧭 Intuition anchor: throughout this chapter, picture VAE as "that hello machine." Every mathematical symbol exists to make this machine formal and trainable.

III. First-principles thinking: what determines handwriting?

The natural thought: handwriting is determined by a small set of hidden factors. From Lecture 2 the instructor lists:

  • The pressure applied when writing
  • Whether the writing slants left or right
  • The letter width (wide / narrow)
  • The writing speed
  • The level of neatness

None of these are visible in the final image, but they completely determine the shape of the writing. So the naive idea is:

  1. Find all the hidden factors that affect handwriting.
  2. Learn a mapping f(hidden factors) → handwriting image.
  3. Feed any combination of hidden factors into $f$ and get a piece of handwriting.

The instructor gives this set of hidden factors a vivid name: "secret recipe."

If a student's secret recipe is "slightly right-leaning, thin strokes, medium speed, medium neatness," their hello is almost completely determined.

Picture this as hardware: imagine an old-school machine with five knobs on the front panel (one per hidden factor), each settable to low/medium/high. You set the knobs, press the button, the machine prints a paper — the hello corresponding to that recipe.

This machine that takes a secret recipe and outputs an image has a formal name in VAE: the Decoder.

IV. Latent Variables: formalising "secret recipe"

The instructor introduces the term:

secret recipe = latent variables, denoted $z$.

Why "latent"? Because they are hidden, invisible factors — but they govern the observed data.

Key convention: for the rest of the lecture, to keep things visualisable, assume only two latent variables $z_1, z_2$:

  • $z_1$ = slantness
  • $z_2$ = neatness
⚠️ In reality we don't know what $z$ is. In the hello example we pretend to know the semantics of $z$ (slant + neat) to build intuition. In a real problem the network learns $z$ itself, and what each dimension of $z$ "means" semantically is usually uninterpretable. That's both the strength and the weakness of neural networks.

4.1 Why "latent"?

The instructor uses a diving metaphor: like a diver descending to the seabed and surfacing again:

  • You want to generate something on the surface (the image);
  • But first you have to dive down to find the "undercurrents" that produce it (the latent variables);
  • Then surface back up with that information.

Generative modeling is essentially "probing the depths" — finding the latent factors that drive surface-level variation.

4.2 Visualising the 2-D latent space

Set $z_1$ on the x-axis and $z_2$ on the y-axis. We get a 2-D plane:

  • Every point on the plane corresponds to a specific handwriting style.
  • Right side of the x-axis → right-leaning; left side → left-leaning.
  • Further from the origin on the y-axis → messier; close to origin → neater.
  • 100 students → 100 points on the plane; the distribution of those 100 points is the probability distribution over the latent space.

An important observation:

A 28×28 hello image has 784 pixel values. We compressed it down to two numbers ($z_1, z_2$).

This is the other face of VAE: dimensionality reduction / representation learning. The latent space is usually far lower-dimensional than the data space (784 → 2 is a 392× compression).

💡 The instructor's foreshadowing: if you plot the 100 students in latent space, they roughly cluster around the origin in an ellipsoid. This hints at: the true latent factors of handwriting plausibly follow a Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ (most people are average, extremes are rare). This observation becomes VAE's prior assumption $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ — enforced via the KL term.

V. Inside the decoder: from latent to pixels

We've drawn the decoder's "interface": input $z$ (2-D), output image (784-D). But what actually happens inside the machine?

5.1 Pixel-level representation

A digital image = 28 × 28 = 784 pixel values, each pixel 0 (black) or 1 (white). The most naive decoder:

Take $z$, output 784 0/1 values, deterministically drawing a digit.

But this has a problem: the same $z$ always yields the exact same image. Yet in reality when we use Stable Diffusion or Midjourney, the same prompt produces different images — the output is probabilistic.

5.2 A probabilistic decoder: each pixel outputs a distribution

Upgrade: each pixel doesn't output a single value but a probability distribution (typically Gaussian or Bernoulli).

  • For each pixel $i$, the decoder outputs $(\mu_i, \sigma_i)$;
  • The actual pixel value is sampled from $\mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$;
  • Black pixel → $\mu \approx 0$; white pixel → $\mu \approx 1$; $\sigma$ controls "the allowed grey-scale jitter."

Now the same $z$ can produce slightly different images each draw — much closer to the real generative process.

🛠️ Practical simplification: the instructor points out that in the practical code later, a deterministic decoder (just the mean, no sigma) is used to keep the implementation simple.
• Deterministic decoder: 784 outputs
• Probabilistic decoder: 784 × 2 = 1568 outputs
For vanilla MNIST VAE the simplification is fine. But the probabilistic framing is still kept formally in the ELBO derivation.

5.3 Decoder = neural network

Now the decoder's essence is clear: it's a neural network $f_\theta(z) \to x$, with $\theta$ as the network's parameters.

  • Input: a low-dim latent (say 2-D);
  • Hidden layers: a few fully connected layers;
  • Output: a high-dim pixel image (784-D).

Formal notation:

$$p_\theta(x|z)$$

Meaning: "given the latent $z$, the probability of producing image $x$." This is the decoder distribution.

VI. Why do we need an encoder? Finding a "map" of latent space

We've been saying "assume the digit 5 lies in some region of latent space" — but where does this assumption come from?

How do you know the digit 5 is on the top-left of the latent space and not the bottom-right?

If you don't know this, generating a 5 means:

  • Brute-force scanning every point in the latent space;
  • Feeding each point into the decoder;
  • Checking which points produce images that look like 5.

That's completely intractable — like fishing for a needle in the ocean.

The encoder's job: given a real image $x$ (say a 5), the encoder directly tells us the region in latent space most likely to have produced this image.

Instead of "carpet bombing," we get "follow the map."

6.1 Encoder = a neural network in the reverse direction

$$q_\phi(z|x)$$
  • Input: image $x$ (784-D);
  • Output: parameters of the latent distribution $(\mu, \sigma^2)$;
  • $\phi$ is the encoder's parameter set.
📝 P and Q notation convention:
• $p_\theta(x|z)$ = decoder (parameters $\theta$): given latent, produce image.
• $q_\phi(z|x)$ = encoder (parameters $\phi$): given image, infer latent.
The order looks "reversed" because P is the true generative direction ($z \to x$), and Q is the encoder's learnt approximate posterior ($x \to z$). These letters are the standard VAE / variational-inference convention.

6.2 Why does the encoder output a "distribution" rather than a "point"? This is where "Variational" comes from

This is the chapter's most-easily-skipped key point.

Plain autoencoder: encoder gives each image a single point.

  • 5 → $(-3, 2)$
  • 1 → $(3, -3)$
  • 2 → $(\ldots)$

Problem: the space between the points is empty! If you sample at $(0, 0)$ — some "middle ground" — and feed it to the decoder, the output is almost always garbage noise. The latent space is discrete and disconnected; you can't freely sample new examples from it.

Variational Autoencoder: encoder gives each image a region (a distribution).

  • 5 → $\mathcal{N}(\mu_5, \Sigma_5)$, a region "where 5s are likely to live";
  • 1 → $\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \Sigma_1)$, another region.

These regions overlap, and points in the overlap could decode to a 5 or a 1 (or a blend). There are no gaps between the regions — the latent space becomes continuous.

"Variational" comes from this: the encoder outputs a variational distribution rather than a fixed point.

6.3 Why is this step so critical?

  • You can generate new samples: sampling any point in latent space gives a reasonable image (no more garbage).
  • Smooth interpolation: walking along a line in latent space gives a smooth transition from 5 to 1.
  • Prevents overfitting: the encoder can't memorise each image to a single point; it has to learn the structure "nearby images should live in nearby latent regions."

VII. Probabilistic view: the latent-variable model

Switching to the probability-theory view, to set up the ELBO derivation.

7.1 The basic setup

Joint distribution:

$$p(x, z) = p(x|z) \, p(z)$$

Where:

  • $p(z)$ = prior: usually $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. That's why a trained VAE's latent space is spherical Gaussian.
  • $p(x|z)$ = likelihood: the probability of generating $x$ given $z$ — modelled by the decoder.

The objective is to maximise the marginal likelihood of data:

$$p(x) = \int p(x|z) \, p(z) \, dz$$

Intuition: enumerate all possible latents $z$, weight each by how likely the decoder thinks it produces $x$, and sum (weighted by how likely each $z$ is to occur).

7.2 Optimisation target

Over the entire dataset $D = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}$, we want:

$$\max_\theta \prod_{i=1}^N p_\theta(x_i) \;\Leftrightarrow\; \max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(x_i)$$

This is maximum likelihood estimation. The instructor anchors the intuition with a counterexample:

If you put a real training image into the decoder and it tells you "$p = 0.001$," the decoder is clearly broken — this image is literally from the true distribution, yet the model thinks it's almost impossible.

So we want to maximise $\log p(x)$.

VIII. Why is $\log p(x)$ hard to compute?

$$\log p(x) = \log \int p(x|z) \, p(z) \, dz$$

This integral is intractable in high dimensions. Concrete obstacles:

  1. The curse of dimensionality: if $z$ is 100-D, this is a 100-D integral. Even with numerical integration, slicing each axis into 10 segments requires $10^{100}$ samples.
  2. No analytic form: $p(x|z)$ is a neural network — no closed form.
  3. Monte Carlo sampling explodes in variance: if you try $z \sim p(z)$ for a Monte Carlo estimate: $$p(x) \approx \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K p(x|z_k), \quad z_k \sim p(z)$$ you'll find almost every $z$ sampled from $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ cannot decode to $x$ (their $p(x|z) \approx 0$). Effective sample size is tiny; variance explodes.
Instructor verbatim: "We can't integrate over the entire universe."

8.1 Can Bayes' rule rescue us?

In theory:

$$p(z|x) = \frac{p(x|z) \, p(z)}{p(x)}$$

But $p(x)$ sits in the denominator! Computing $p(z|x)$ is exactly as hard as computing $p(x)$. So the "true posterior" $p(z|x)$ is also intractable.

This is where variational inference enters.

IX. Variational inference: an approximate posterior

9.1 Core idea

Since the true posterior $p(z|x)$ is intractable, use a parameterisable, easy-to-sample distribution $q_\phi(z|x)$ to approximate it. Typically:

$$q_\phi(z|x) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(z; \mu_\phi(x), \sigma^2_\phi(x)\big)$$

Where $\mu_\phi(x), \sigma_\phi(x)$ are outputs of the encoder neural network. The encoder is literally the variational approximation.

9.2 The pivotal algebraic identity

The next step is the linchpin of variational inference — read it three times:

$$\log p(x) = \underbrace{\mathrm{ELBO}(x)}_{\text{computable}} + \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z|x)\big)}_{\geq 0,\text{ not computable}}$$

$\log p(x)$ equals ELBO + a non-negative KL term. So:

$$\log p(x) \geq \mathrm{ELBO}(x)$$

ELBO is the lower bound on $\log p(x)$ — hence the name Evidence Lower BOund ($\log p(x)$ is the "evidence").

The crucial insight:

  • We can't maximise $\log p(x)$ directly (intractable);
  • But we can maximise the ELBO (tractable);
  • Maximising ELBO does two good things at once:
    1. Pushes the lower bound of $\log p(x)$ higher → indirectly maximises $\log p(x)$;
    2. Shrinks $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p_\text{true})$ → the encoder approximates the true posterior better.
The instructor's red-green diagram: "Imagine the green curve is $\log p(x)$ (unreachable). The red curve is the ELBO — always below green. As long as you push red up, green can't be lower than red."

X. Full ELBO derivation (one step per line, with annotations)

The most important math of this chapter. Every step has a "why."

Step 1: Write out $\log p(x)$ $$\log p(x) = \log \int p(x|z) \, p(z) \, dz$$ Why: directly using the definition of $p(x)$ via marginalisation.
Step 2: Multiply by 1 (the importance-sampling trick) $$= \log \int p(x|z) \, p(z) \cdot \frac{q_\phi(z|x)}{q_\phi(z|x)} \, dz \;=\; \log \int q_\phi(z|x) \cdot \frac{p(x|z) \, p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)} \, dz$$ Why: multiplying by $q/q = 1$ doesn't change the integral, but lets us rewrite it as an expectation over $q$: $$= \log \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\frac{p(x|z) \, p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\right]$$ This converts "integral over $z$" into "expectation under samples from $q$" — and $q$ is something we can sample from (a Gaussian).
Step 3: Apply Jensen's inequality For the concave function $\log$, we have $\log \mathbb{E}[Y] \geq \mathbb{E}[\log Y]$: $$\geq \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x|z) \, p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\right]$$ Why: log is concave, so "log of expectation" $\geq$ "expectation of log." This step moves the log inside the integral, letting us split it open later.
Cost: we turned equality into inequality — the lost slack is exactly $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \| p_\text{true})$ later.
Step 4: Split the log $$= \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log p(x|z) + \log p(z) - \log q_\phi(z|x)\right]$$ Why: $\log(abc) = \log a + \log b + \log c$. Standard log arithmetic.
Step 5: Regroup $$= \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] + \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\right]$$ $$= \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] - \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log \frac{q_\phi(z|x)}{p(z)}\right]$$ Why: bundle the two prior-related terms into one log-ratio.
Step 6: Recognise the KL divergence $$\boxed{\log p(x) \geq \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] \;-\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z)\big)}$$ Why: the second term is exactly the KL-divergence definition: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \| p) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log(q/p)]$. Importantly, this KL is between $q$ and the prior $p(z)$, not the true posterior $p(z|x)$ — so it is computable!

That's the ELBO:

$$\mathrm{ELBO} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z)\big)}_{\text{regularisation}}$$

XI. What the two ELBO terms mean (engrave them in memory)

11.1 ① Reconstruction term

"Pass $x$ through the encoder to get $z$, then through the decoder to get $x'$. We want $x' \approx x$."
  • Sample a $z$ from $q_\phi(z|x)$ (the encoder's output);
  • Compute $\log p_\theta(x|z)$ — "how likely the decoder thinks the original image $x$ is, given this $z$";
  • We want this value high → the decoder reconstructs $x$ accurately.

In practice for images, this is implemented as a pixel-level loss:

  • Continuous pixel values → Gaussian likelihood → equivalent to MSE loss;
  • 0/1 binary pixels → Bernoulli likelihood → equivalent to binary cross-entropy (BCE) — which is what the instructor uses on MNIST.

11.2 ② Regularisation term

"The encoder's output distribution $q_\phi(z|x)$ should stay close to the prior $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$."
  • This term measures the "distance" between the encoder's output and a spherical Gaussian.
  • Without it, the encoder would cheat: map every image to a narrow $\delta$-distribution ($\sigma \to 0$) and the decoder would memorise. The latent space would degenerate back to discrete points — i.e. plain autoencoder.
  • The KL term forces the encoder to output broader distributions centred near the origin → distributions of different images overlap → the latent space becomes continuous.
  • Also, because the entire latent space is close to $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, to generate new samples we just sample $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and decode — this is how VAE produces samples from "nothing."

11.3 The Earth-to-Mars cat teleportation

From Lecture 2, an unusually vivid metaphor:

Say you want to teleport a cat from Earth to Mars. Sending every atom is impossible (too much data). So you compress the cat into a "recipe" $z$ (the latent), transmit it, and decode it on Mars.

• Reconstruction goal: the cat decoded on Mars should look like the cat on Earth.
• Regularisation goal: the transmitted recipe must use a "standard language" — namely $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$. Otherwise the receivers on Mars can't read it.

XII. The reparameterisation trick

We have a loss (–ELBO). The next step is backpropagating gradients. But there's a sticking point.

12.1 The problem: sampling blocks gradients

Inside the ELBO we have to "sample $z$ from $q_\phi(z|x)$" and then feed $z$ into the decoder.

  • $q_\phi$'s $\mu, \sigma$ are produced by the encoder (parameters $\phi$);
  • We want $\partial \mathrm{ELBO} / \partial \phi$;
  • But the "sample" operation is non-differentiable — it's a random operator with no gradient flowing from $z$ back to $\mu, \sigma$.

12.2 The fix: squeeze the randomness out

Rewrite $z$ as:

$$z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

(where $\odot$ is element-wise multiplication)

The beauty:

  • $z$ is now a deterministic function of $\mu, \sigma$ (once $\epsilon$ is given);
  • All randomness is packed into $\epsilon$, which is parameter-independent;
  • $\partial z / \partial \mu = 1$, $\partial z / \partial \sigma = \epsilon$, so gradients flow back to the encoder cleanly.

12.3 In code

mu, log_var = encoder(x)        # encoder outputs μ and log(σ²)
std = torch.exp(0.5 * log_var)  # σ = exp(0.5 * log σ²)
eps = torch.randn_like(std)     # ε ~ N(0, I)
z = mu + std * eps              # reparameterised sample
🧰 Why does the encoder output $\log(\sigma^2)$ instead of $\sigma$?
• $\sigma$ must be $> 0$, but neural network outputs can be any real number.
• Letting the network output $\log(\sigma^2) \in \mathbb{R}$ (unconstrained) and then exponentiating guarantees positivity and numerical stability.
• When you see log_var in real VAE code, this is why.

12.4 The same trick is used in DDPM

Every step of DDPM denoising uses the reparameterisation trick to sample. This is not a VAE-specific trick — it's a universal tool for all "generative models with random variables that need to be differentiable." See the concept page: reparameterisation trick.

XIII. The VAE loss function (practical)

Negate the ELBO to get a loss to minimise:

$$\boxed{L_{\text{VAE}} = \underbrace{-\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]}_{\text{reconstruction loss}} \;+\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z)\big)}_{\text{KL regularisation}}}$$

13.1 Closed-form KL (Gaussian–Gaussian)

When $q_\phi(z|x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, the KL has a closed form:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) \,\|\, \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\big) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^d \big(\mu_i^2 + \sigma_i^2 - \log \sigma_i^2 - 1\big)$$

where $d$ is the latent dimension. Term-by-term:

  • $\mu_i^2$: penalises means away from 0 (centres the distribution).
  • $\sigma_i^2$: penalises too-wide $\sigma$ (prevents the distribution from spreading out).
  • $-\log \sigma_i^2$: penalises too-small $\sigma$ (prevents the distribution from collapsing to a point).
  • $-1$: constant; ensures KL is exactly 0 when $\mu = 0, \sigma = 1$.

13.2 Two flavours of reconstruction loss

  • MNIST (binary pixels): BCE 
    recon_loss = F.binary_cross_entropy(x_recon, x, reduction='sum')
  • Natural images (continuous pixels): MSE 
    recon_loss = F.mse_loss(x_recon, x, reduction='sum')
⚠️ Why sum and not mean: the KL term gives one scalar per sample; the reconstruction term must sum over all pixels per sample to match the dimensional balance. Otherwise KL ends up several orders of magnitude smaller than reconstruction and gets ignored.

This is the classic VAE training pitfall — KL term gets drowned out → the model degenerates into a plain autoencoder. The fix is either sum, or weight the KL up (β-VAE).

XIV. PyTorch implementation skeleton

A minimal runnable MNIST VAE, demonstrating every concept above in code:

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class VAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, x_dim=784, h_dim=400, z_dim=2):
        super().__init__()
        # ===== Encoder: 784 -> 400 -> (2 mu, 2 log_var) =====
        self.fc1 = nn.Linear(x_dim, h_dim)
        self.fc_mu = nn.Linear(h_dim, z_dim)
        self.fc_log_var = nn.Linear(h_dim, z_dim)
        # ===== Decoder: 2 -> 400 -> 784 =====
        self.fc2 = nn.Linear(z_dim, h_dim)
        self.fc3 = nn.Linear(h_dim, x_dim)

    def encode(self, x):
        h = F.relu(self.fc1(x))
        return self.fc_mu(h), self.fc_log_var(h)

    def reparameterize(self, mu, log_var):
        # Reparameterisation trick: z = mu + sigma * epsilon
        std = torch.exp(0.5 * log_var)
        eps = torch.randn_like(std)
        return mu + std * eps

    def decode(self, z):
        h = F.relu(self.fc2(z))
        return torch.sigmoid(self.fc3(h))  # squash output to [0, 1]

    def forward(self, x):
        mu, log_var = self.encode(x.view(-1, 784))
        z = self.reparameterize(mu, log_var)
        return self.decode(z), mu, log_var


def vae_loss(x_recon, x, mu, log_var):
    recon = F.binary_cross_entropy(x_recon, x.view(-1, 784), reduction='sum')
    # KL = -0.5 * sum(1 + log_var - mu^2 - exp(log_var))
    kl = -0.5 * torch.sum(1 + log_var - mu.pow(2) - log_var.exp())
    return recon + kl
🟡 Author's note (looking ahead to Stable Diffusion): a vanilla VAE in 2026 looks "old" — but it didn't die. It became infrastructure. Every latent-diffusion model relies on a pretrained VAE to bridge "high-resolution image ↔ latent" — the diffusion process operates in the latent space. Every line of encoder / decoder code you just wrote is a simplified version of the front-end and back-end of Stable Diffusion's pipeline (we get to that in Chapter 18).

What to commit to memory (the instructor's anchors)

  1. VAE = "build a machine that samples" (the hello machine). Press a button, get a new sample — its distribution should match the training data.
  2. "Secret recipe" = latent variable $z$. Find hidden factors → build a $z \to x$ mapping → the sampling problem is half-solved.
  3. Plain AE gives "points"; VAE gives "distributions" — this single change turns the latent space continuous and lets us sample anywhere.
  4. $\log p(x)$ is intractable → ELBO is its lower bound (the instructor's Earth-to-Mars cat-teleportation metaphor). Jensen's inequality + the KL split give the 6-step derivation.
  5. ELBO = reconstruction − KL. Reconstruction makes the decoder match the original; KL forces the encoder's output close to $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
  6. The reparameterisation trick $z = \mu + \sigma \epsilon$ makes random sampling differentiable — VAE's training lifeblood, and reused throughout DDPM.
  7. Engineering details: network outputs $\log \sigma^2$ (positivity + numerical stability); use sum not mean to keep dimensional balance; MNIST uses BCE, natural images use MSE; reconstruction tests (encode → decode the same image) are a diagnostic independent of generation — if reconstruction fails, sampling will definitely fail.
