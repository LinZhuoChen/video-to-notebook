3. ELBO: The Evidence Lower Bound

Primary source: Vizuara Principles of Diffusion Models Lecture 2 (Dr. Raj Dandekar); secondary: CMU 10-799 S26 Lecture 1 (Kelly Hu). This chapter cracks open the step from the previous chapter — "$\log p(x)$ is intractable → replace with ELBO" — because it is the parent template for every diffusion loss in the rest of the textbook.

📌 TL;DR

ELBO (Evidence Lower BOund) is a quantity we can compute, used as a proxy for the quantity we want but cannot compute — $\log p(x)$.

Core inequality:

$$\log p(x) \;\geq\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(z|x) \,\|\, p(z)\big)}_{\text{KL regulariser}} \;=\; \mathrm{ELBO}(x)$$

The proof needs only three moves: (1) multiply by $q/q = 1$ inside the integral to rewrite it as an expectation under $q$; (2) apply Jensen's inequality to push $\log$ inside the expectation; (3) split the log and recognise the prior-related part as KL divergence.

Three names to memorise: $\log p(x)$ is called the evidence; ELBO is its lower bound; the gap between them is exactly $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, \text{true posterior})$. So pushing ELBO up does two things at once — approximate the true log-likelihood, and push the variational $q$ toward the true posterior.

Why read this chapter: VAE training (last chapter) is literally ELBO maximisation; DDPM training (starting Chapter 5) is ELBO unrolled across $T$ steps. Every "scary-looking" diffusion loss formula later has these three lines as its spine. We also lay down one meta-line: ELBO is not some alien — it belongs to the maximum-likelihood-estimation lineage, just the version we have to fall back on when $p(x)$ itself is intractable.

I. Meta: every loss function is some flavour of maximum likelihood

Before we plunge into ELBO's algebraic gymnastics, pause and ask a simpler question: why do we want to compute $\log p(x)$ in the first place? Why not just use MSE, CrossEntropy, BCE — the more "engineering-feeling" losses?

The unifying answer: all of those are maximum-likelihood estimation in disguise.

  • Regression with MSE = MLE under "model output is the mean of a Gaussian": $-\log \mathcal{N}(y; \mu_\theta(x), \sigma^2)$ unrolls to a constant plus $\|y - \mu_\theta(x)\|^2$.
  • Binary classification with BCE = MLE under "model output is a Bernoulli probability."
  • Language modelling with cross-entropy = MLE under "next-token is categorical."
  • Generative modelling? Theoretically we'd also like to maximise $\log p_\theta(x)$. The problem: when the model contains a latent variable $z$, $p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x|z) p(z) \, dz$ is intractable in high dimensions. That's the entire reason ELBO exists.
🟡 Author's note: this "every loss is MLE" view gives the chapter a psychological anchor — we're not learning some weird new thing, we're using MLE to solve a degenerate case of MLE. $\log p_\theta(x)$ is what we genuinely want to maximise; ELBO is its "engineering-computable proxy." Once you internalise that, the VAE loss from last chapter, the ELBO derived here, and the DDPM $L_{\text{simple}}$ in Chapter 5 line up as the same stick — all variants of "MLE under a latent-variable structure."

II. Why detour through all this? Can't we maximise $\log p(x)$ directly?

Every generative model's "ideal target" is straightforward to write:

$$\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(x_i)$$

Given a training set, tune $\theta$ so the model assigns high probability to real data. If we could do this directly the world would be wonderful. But once the model has a latent variable $z$,

$$p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x|z) \, p(z) \, dz$$

this integral is intractable in high dimensions. Three reasons (recap from last chapter):

  1. Curse of dimensionality. $z$ is typically tens to hundreds of dimensions; the sample count for numerical integration explodes exponentially.
  2. No analytic form. $p_\theta(x|z)$ is a neural network; no closed-form anti-derivative.
  3. Naive Monte Carlo has explosive variance. Sampling $z_k \sim p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and averaging $p(x|z_k)$ — most $z$ sampled from the prior don't decode to $x$ at all ($p(x|z_k) \approx 0$), so effective sample size is tiny.
Dr. Raj's recurring framing in Lecture 2: "We can't integrate over the entire universe." We cannot enumerate every possible $z$.

2.1 Feel the "intractable" with a number

"High-dim integral can't be computed" goes numb after hearing it enough — here's a concrete number. Suppose $z$ is 100-D (typical VAE scale). For the naivest numerical integration, slicing each dimension into 10 steps requires $10^{100}$ grid points. The number of particles in the universe is about $10^{80}$. In other words, using every particle in the universe as a grid point isn't enough. This isn't "slow" — it's "physically impossible." So we must find another path.

2.2 The Earth-to-Mars cat metaphor (now repurposed)

From Lecture 2, Dr. Raj's vivid story about why $p(x)$ is intractable and why we must find a substitute to optimise:

Say you want to teleport a cat from Earth to Mars. Sending every atom is impossible. So you compress the cat into a "recipe" $z$ (the latent), transmit it, and decode it on Mars.

• Reconstruction goal: the cat decoded on Mars should look like the cat on Earth.
• Regularisation goal: the recipe must use a "standard language" — namely $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ — or the receivers can't read it.

Adding an ELBO-flavoured layer to this metaphor:

  • "The probability the cat actually exists" $p(x)$ is like "the cat materialising fully and simultaneously on the Martian surface." That probability is universe-scale tiny — uncomputable.
  • What we can compute is: given a recipe $z$, the probability of decoding into this cat $p(x|z)$, plus whether the recipe is well-formed $p(z)$ versus $q(z|x)$.
  • Combining the two computable quantities (with the right signs and expectations) gives ELBO — a proxy objective whose expression we can write down, take gradients of, and backpropagate through.
🧭 All the magic of this chapter in one sentence: since $p(x)$ is intractable, find a quantity $L$ such that $L \leq \log p(x)$ and $L$ is tractable. Maximise $L$ — "lifting the lower bound" indirectly lifts $\log p(x)$ too. ELBO is that $L$.

III. CMU side: Kelly Hu calls this "the greatest mathematical trick of all time"

Vizuara approaches the derivation intuition-first (hello machine → maths). CMU 10-799's instructor Kelly Hu takes a nearly reverent tone toward the same algebraic move. She calls the step "multiply by $q/q$ inside the integral, then apply Jensen" "the greatest mathematical trick of all time."

Kelly (paraphrased): when you see $\log p(x) = \log \int p(x, z) \, dz$ and it's intractable, don't try to attack the integral head-on. Multiply by 1, turn the integral into an expectation, push the log inside — this is the entire reason the field of variational inference exists.

This chapter unpacks the "greatest trick" into six steps, each with a "why."

IV. Notation refresher

Every letter in the ELBO derivation comes from the VAE chapter, but in case you skipped it:

  • $x$: observed data (e.g. a handwritten "hello" image).
  • $z$: latent variable (secret recipe).
  • $p(z)$: prior, typically $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$. "How latents are distributed before seeing any image."
  • $p_\theta(x|z)$: likelihood / decoder with parameters $\theta$. "Given recipe $z$, the probability of producing image $x$."
  • $p(x)$: marginal likelihood / evidence. $p(x) = \int p(x|z) p(z) \, dz$. The quantity we want to maximise. Intractable.
  • $p(z|x)$: true posterior. By Bayes, $p(z|x) = \frac{p(x|z)p(z)}{p(x)}$ — but $p(x)$ in the denominator makes it equally intractable.
  • $q_\phi(z|x)$: variational approximate posterior / encoder, parameters $\phi$. Its job is to approximate $p(z|x)$. Typically $q_\phi(z|x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(x), \sigma^2_\phi(x))$.
⚠️ The two KLs that get confused all the time:
• $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z))$ — q vs prior, tractable, closed-form for Gaussian-Gaussian. This is the ELBO's regularisation term.
• $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z|x))$ — q vs true posterior, intractable (because $p(z|x)$ is intractable). This is the gap between $\log p(x)$ and ELBO.
Same numerator-denominator shape, different second term — one tiny pipe character apart and the chapter falls apart.

V. The full ELBO derivation (six steps, each with Why)

The heart of this chapter. If you can close the book and reproduce these six lines, you understand ELBO.

Step 1: Start from the definition of evidence $$\log p(x) \;=\; \log \int p(x, z) \, dz \;=\; \log \int p(x|z) \, p(z) \, dz$$ Why: $p(x)$ is $x$'s marginal — write it as an integral over the joint $p(x, z) = p(x|z) p(z)$, then take log because we want to maximise log-likelihood.
Difficulty: this integral is intractable in high-dim $z$.
Step 2: Multiply by $q(z|x) / q(z|x) = 1$ — Kelly's "greatest mathematical trick" $$= \log \int p(x|z) \, p(z) \cdot \frac{q_\phi(z|x)}{q_\phi(z|x)} \, dz \;=\; \log \int q_\phi(z|x) \cdot \frac{p(x|z) \, p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)} \, dz$$ Why: multiplying by "itself over itself" inside the integral doesn't change the value, but the structure becomes "density $q_\phi(z|x)$ times a function." That lets us rewrite the integral as an expectation under $q$ (which we can sample from). $$= \log \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\frac{p(x|z) \, p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\right]$$ Note: every move so far is an equality. No information lost yet.
Step 3: Apply Jensen's inequality $\log$ is concave. For a concave $f$ and random variable $Y$, Jensen says $f(\mathbb{E}[Y]) \geq \mathbb{E}[f(Y)]$. With $f = \log$: $$\log \mathbb{E}[Y] \geq \mathbb{E}[\log Y]$$ Applied here: $$\log p(x) \;\geq\; \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x|z) \, p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\right]$$ Why: moving $\log$ from "outside the expectation" to "inside" is the qualitative leap — inside, we can split the log and identify familiar KL structure; outside, the log on an expectation is opaque.
Cost: equality becomes inequality. The lost slack is exactly $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z|x))$ — proven in §VI.
Step 4: Split the log $$= \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\!\big[\log p(x|z) + \log p(z) - \log q_\phi(z|x)\big]$$ Why: $\log(abc) = \log a + \log b + \log c$, and $\log(a/b) = \log a - \log b$. Pure algebra.
ELBO is now "showing" — the first term is the decoder term; the remaining two combine into a KL.
Step 5: Bundle the prior-related terms $$= \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] \;+\; \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\right]$$ $$= \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] \;-\; \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\!\left[\log \frac{q_\phi(z|x)}{p(z)}\right]$$ Why: the second term is "expectation under $q$ of $\log(q/p)$" — exactly the form of KL divergence.
Step 6: Recognise the KL — write ELBO $$\boxed{\;\log p(x) \;\geq\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)]}_{\text{recon}} \;-\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z)\big)}_{\text{KL regulariser}} \;=\; \mathrm{ELBO}(x)\;}$$ Why: by definition $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \| p) := \mathbb{E}_q[\log(q/p)]$.
Key point: this KL is between $q$ and the prior $p(z)$ (e.g. $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$), not the true posterior $p(z|x)$. The former has a closed form between two Gaussians, tractable. The latter is not. This is exactly why ELBO is computable.
💡 Section III's "greatest trick" = Steps 2 + 3.
  • Step 2 packages the intractable integral as an expectation under $q$ — looks like "multiplying by 1, doing nothing," yet opens every door downstream.
  • Step 3 uses Jensen to push $\log$ inside the expectation — pays a cost (inequality) in exchange for a decomposable form.
Together they constitute the entire technical core of variational inference. Every diffusion or score-based model's loss derivation is basically these two moves applied on different probability structures.

VI. Why is $\log p(x) - \mathrm{ELBO} = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p_{\text{true posterior}})$?

Step 3 paid a cost — how big is the $\geq$ gap? The answer is a beautiful identity:

$$\log p(x) \;=\; \mathrm{ELBO}(x) \;+\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z|x)\big)$$

Short proof. Start from the definition of KL divergence:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(z|x) \| p(z|x)\big) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log \frac{q(z|x)}{p(z|x)}\right]$$

Substitute Bayes $p(z|x) = p(x|z) p(z) / p(x)$ in the denominator:

$$= \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log \frac{q(z|x) \, p(x)}{p(x|z) \, p(z)}\right]$$

Split the log and pull $\log p(x)$ out of the expectation (it doesn't depend on $z$):

$$= \log p(x) \;-\; \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x|z)] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z|x) \| p(z))\big)}_{= \mathrm{ELBO}(x)}$$

So $\log p(x) = \mathrm{ELBO}(x) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p_{\text{true posterior}})$. KL is non-negative, hence $\log p(x) \geq \mathrm{ELBO}(x)$. Equality holds iff $q(z|x) = p(z|x)$ — the variational approximation perfectly matches the true posterior.

💡 This identity says: maximising ELBO does two things at once:
  1. $\log p(x)$ is a fixed number for a given $x$, so "pushing ELBO up" $\Leftrightarrow$ "shrinking $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \| p_{\text{true posterior}})$" — i.e. training the encoder to better approximate the true posterior.
  2. Since $\log p(x) \geq \mathrm{ELBO}$, pushing ELBO up raises the "ceiling that $\log p(x)$ has to clear" — indirectly training the decoder so the data likelihood increases.
One loss does two jobs — the elegance that makes variational inference beautiful.

VII. ELBO on a number line: Jensen's "lift"

Push the green solid (ELBO) up and the blue dashed (true log-likelihood) is at least as high. With enough variational flexibility and training, the gap can shrink to nearly zero. But the gap is always $\geq 0$ in theory: ELBO is a lower bound, not an equivalent.

VIII. The physical meaning of the two terms

Negate ELBO to get the loss minimised at training:

$$L_{\text{VAE}}(x) \;=\; -\,\mathrm{ELBO}(x) \;=\; \underbrace{-\,\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]}_{\text{reconstruction loss}} \;+\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z)\big)}_{\text{KL regularisation}}$$

8.1 Reconstruction term

Pass real $x$ through the encoder → sample $z$ → feed $z$ to the decoder → maximise "how likely the decoder thinks the original $x$ is." In practice:

  • $p_\theta(x|z) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(z), \sigma^2 I)$ → $-\log p_\theta(x|z)$ simplifies to MSE.
  • $p_\theta(x|z) = \prod_i \text{Bernoulli}(\mu_i)$ → $-\log p_\theta(x|z)$ simplifies to binary cross-entropy.
🟡 Author's note (echoing §I): MSE and BCE reappear here as the specific MLE forms inside the decoder's conditional distribution. Full chain: we want MLE → latent makes $p(x)$ intractable → fall back to ELBO → ELBO's reconstruction simplifies back to MSE/BCE, the MLE under the decoder's conditional. MLE recurses through the chapter — every "can't compute it" wraps a variational proxy until it bottoms out at a tractable MLE.

8.2 KL regularisation

$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z|x) \,\|\, p(z))$ measures the distance between the encoder's output distribution and the prior.

  • Without it, the encoder cheats — map every image to a hyper-narrow $\delta$-distribution and let the decoder memorise. The latent space degenerates back to disconnected points.
  • KL forces $q_\phi(z|x)$ to look like $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ — neither too wide ($\sigma$ too large gets penalised) nor too narrow ($-\log \sigma^2$ explodes), centred near the origin.
  • Result: different images' $q_\phi(z|x)$ overlap; the latent space becomes continuous; sampling any $z$ decodes to a sensible image.

You should now have an "aha — the VAE loss is the negative ELBO" moment.

Chapter 2 "reverse-engineered" the VAE loss from intuition; this chapter "forward-derived" ELBO from $\log p(x)$. They are equivalent. VAE is the simplest concrete instance of the ELBO mathematical structure.

⚠️ Common misconception: "ELBO is VAE-specific."
Correction: ELBO is the universal training objective for any latent-variable probabilistic generative model. VAE is the simplest instance (one latent step). DDPM is another ($T$ latent steps, Gaussian transition kernel, fixed encoder). Score-based and flow-matching models also trace back to ELBO.

X. Forward look: DDPM's loss is just $T$-step ELBO

VAE's latent is one step: $x \to z \to x$. DDPM unrolls into $T$ steps:

$$x = x_0 \;\to\; x_1 \;\to\; x_2 \;\to\; \cdots \;\to\; x_T \approx \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

Each forward step $q(x_t | x_{t-1})$ is fixed (no learnable parameters, just a variance schedule). Each reverse step $p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t)$ is learnable.

The full ELBO looks like (derivation in Chapter 5):

$$\log p(x_0) \;\geq\; \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log p_\theta(x_0 | x_1) \;-\; \sum_{t=2}^T D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t)\big) \;-\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(x_T | x_0) \,\|\, p(x_T)\big)\right]$$

Scary-looking, but the skeleton is VAE's ELBO:

  • Leftmost term — reconstruction ("last step recovers $x_0$ from $x_1$").
  • The big sum of KLs — one per step (VAE has 1; DDPM has $T-1$).
  • Rightmost KL — does $x_T$ end up close to the prior? (Same role as VAE's prior-matching KL.)

10.1 The simple-loss sneak peek

That $T$-term ELBO compresses (via reparameterisation + completing-the-square + a carefully chosen variance) into one line of MSE:

$$L_{\text{simple}}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \epsilon}\Big[\big\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\big\|^2\Big]$$

"Why does such a complex ELBO collapse to a noise-prediction MSE?" Because the forward chain is Gaussian, and every-step KL between two Gaussians simplifies to "squared distance between two means." This is Ho et al. 2020's engineering contribution. The simplification starts from this chapter's ELBO. Echoing §I: this MSE is also "MLE under a conditional Gaussian" — MLE recurses.

XI. ELBO as a general optimisation paradigm

ELBO isn't just a formula; it's a general recipe for "I want to compute X but X is intractable":

  1. Identify what you actually want (here: $\log p(x)$).
  2. Acknowledge it's intractable; isolate the culprit (high-dim integral).
  3. Introduce an auxiliary distribution $q$ (easy to sample / parameterise).
  4. Construct a tractable lower (or upper) bound.
  5. Gradient-optimise the proxy.

The same pattern recurs through ML — EM algorithm, variational inference, every diffusion/score-based training objective.

XII. Common misconceptions

12.1 "ELBO is an inequality — what exactly is optimised at train time?"

ELBO itself (negated for gradient descent). We never directly optimise $\log p(x)$; we optimise its computable lower bound, which is a well-defined function of $\theta, \phi$.

12.2 "Why use KL($q \| p$) not KL($p \| q$)?"

KL is asymmetric. ELBO naturally yields forward KL, which is mode-seeking — if the true posterior is multimodal, $q$ tends to cover only one mode. This is a known "feature" of variational inference. Diffusion uses forward KL for engineering tractability.

12.3 "When is Jensen tight?"

Iff $Y$ is constant under the relevant distribution. In our derivation $Y = p(x,z)/q(z|x)$. Constant in $z$ under $q$ means $q(z|x) = p(z|x)$ — same condition as the §VI identity. Training the encoder to better approximate the true posterior and tightening ELBO are the same task.

XIII. (Optional) ELBO from an information-theory angle

Regrouped:

$$\mathrm{ELBO}(x) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] \;-\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z|x) \| p(z))$$
  • First term: decoder's log-probability of the observation — the negative reconstruction coding cost.
  • Second term: the latent's coding cost — bits to encode $z$ with the prior codebook, minus bits with $q$.

Maximising ELBO equals minimising total coding length — encoding $x$ via $z$ with the fewest bits. This is the rate-distortion / bits-back-coding view, linking VAE to lossless compression. Hinton & Zemel's 1993 paper on autoencoders and minimum description length derives an objective which, in hindsight, is ELBO — so it's been rediscovered many times. ELBO is simultaneously MLE's latent-variable extension and MDL's probabilistic version.

XIV. Source video clips

Seven anchors to take away

  1. Every loss is a flavour of MLE. MSE = Gaussian MLE; BCE = Bernoulli MLE; ELBO = the proxy you fall back on when the latent-variable structure makes $p(x)$ intractable.
  2. $\log p(x)$ is intractable (Dr. Raj's "we can't integrate over the entire universe" / Earth-to-Mars cat). So we maximise its tractable lower bound ELBO.
  3. ELBO = reconstruction $-$ KL. Reconstruction makes the decoder rebuild the image; KL keeps the encoder's $q(z|x)$ close to $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
  4. "The greatest mathematical trick of all time" (Kelly Hu) = multiplying the integral by $q/q$ + Jensen's inequality. These two moves together are variational inference.
  5. The identity $\log p(x) = \mathrm{ELBO}(x) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z|x) \| p(z|x))$. Pushing ELBO up does two jobs (improve likelihood + improve variational approximation).
  6. The KL inside ELBO is q vs the prior, not q vs the true posterior. Former tractable (closed form for two Gaussians); latter not (true posterior is itself intractable). This is the design trick that makes ELBO usable.
  7. VAE = ELBO's simplest instance; DDPM = ELBO unrolled across $T$ steps. §V's six steps + Markov-chain forward + Bayes-reverse = parent template for every diffusion loss to come.
